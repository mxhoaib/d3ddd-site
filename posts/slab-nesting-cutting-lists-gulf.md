Stone is expensive. Freight, customs and remakes make it more expensive. Yet many Gulf packages still treat the **cutting list** as an afterthought — a spreadsheet someone builds after the drawings are “done,” instead of as part of the same documentation set.

This article is a practical look at **slab nesting** and cutting lists for marble, granite and sintered stone work in Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Sharjah and neighbouring markets. No invented waste percentages; just habits that keep yield honest.

## Nesting is not a puzzle game — it is risk management

Nesting means arranging pieces on known slab sizes (and known usable area after defects) so the yard cuts as much saleable work as possible from each slab. Good nesting also protects **visual intent**: book-matched pairs stay together, feature pieces stay off cracked zones, and grain direction stays consistent across a wall.

Bad nesting looks efficient on a PDF and fails on the saw when:

- slab photos were never tied to piece IDs
- the list used nominal sizes without kerf or polish allowance
- “mirror” pieces were flipped the wrong way for vein flow
- a late design change orphaned half a nested sheet

## Build the cutting list from the shop drawings — not the other way around

Start from fabrication-ready [marble shop drawings](/marble.html):

1. **Locked piece schedule** — ID, finish, finished size, quantity, location.
2. **Allowances** — polish / edge stock, kerf, and any factory vs field trim.
3. **Material batches** — which pieces must share the same slab lot for colour match.
4. **Priority** — feature pieces first; fillers and low-visibility returns last.

Only then do you nest onto actual slab inventory (length × width, thickness, defect map). Nesting before the schedule is stable is how yards cut the wrong island twice.

## What a useful cutting list contains

A list a supervisor can print and mark up usually has:

- Piece ID (same as drawings)
- Finished size and cut size
- Edge codes
- Slab ID / bundle / crate reference
- Nest position or sheet number
- Grain / vein arrow
- Notes (“pair with M-12”, “avoid left edge defect”, “do not flip”)

If your list cannot be traced back to an elevation tag in thirty seconds, it will not survive a busy Saturday shift.

## Yield: talk in process, not magic numbers

Clients sometimes ask for a single “waste %” to plug into a [BOQ](/services/architectural-estimation/). Reality varies by:

- slab size vs piece size mix
- how many book-matched pairs you need
- defect rate in that batch
- whether edges are profiled before or after cut
- remake contingency the contractor already priced

A more honest approach for estimation:

- Nest the **known** package against **quoted** slab sizes
- State assumptions (slab size, usable area, kerf)
- Separate **design waste** (geometry) from **contingency** (breakage / remakes)
- Update the nest when the drawing revision changes piece sizes

That is ordinary quantity discipline — the same mindset behind careful [AutoCAD drafting](/services/architectural-drafting/) and take-offs — not a black-box “optimiser” promise.

## Vein direction and book-matching

For feature walls and island tops, nesting is a design tool:

- Keep paired pieces on the same slab when colour match matters
- Show vein arrows on both the nest and the elevation
- Photograph slabs and annotate piece outlines before cutting when the client cares about the look

A perfect geometric nest that ignores vein flow still produces a wall the client rejects — which is the most expensive kind of “efficient.”

## Checklist before the first cut

- [ ] Drawing revision letter matches the cutting list revision
- [ ] Every piece ID appears once on drawings and once on the list
- [ ] Cut sizes include agreed allowances
- [ ] Slab IDs match physical stock in the yard
- [ ] Book-matched pairs and grain arrows reviewed on screen *and* on slab photos
- [ ] “Do not cut” holds remain for pieces awaiting site measure
