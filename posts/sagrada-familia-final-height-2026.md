On **20 February 2026**, a crane set the upper arm of the cross on Barcelona’s **Tower of Jesus Christ**, taking Antoni Gaudí’s Sagrada Família to its designed maximum height of **172.5 metres (566 feet)** — making it the world’s tallest church by that measure ([AP News](https://apnews.com/article/spain-barcelona-gaudi-sagrada-familia-church-ba90e5211913fa954ff63d54dd6efa69); [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/06/08/style/sagrada-familia-barcelona-tower-jesus-christ-intl)). Months later, ceremonial blessings around Gaudí’s death centennial flooded social feeds with a simpler headline: *it’s finished*.

It is not. The basilica’s own progress communication still describes construction at about **70%**, with multiple fronts open — including work inside the new tower — and no firm final completion date ([Gaudí Museum explainer](https://gaudi.museum/en/news/is-the-sagrada-familia-finished/); [Domus](https://www.domusweb.it/en/news/2026/07/15/sagrada-familia-completion-demolitions-barcelona.html)).

For anyone who produces drawings for stone, façades or long-running cultural projects, 2026 is a masterclass in **how milestones get misread**.

## What actually completed in 2026

What finished externally is the **central tower’s exterior** at final height. Technical notes circulated with the cross describe a large glazed-ceramic and glass terminal — on the order of **17 m high by 13.5 m wide** within a taller spire assembly — installed in sections after fabrication abroad ([Gaudí Museum](https://gaudi.museum/en/news/is-the-sagrada-familia-finished/); [Gaudí centennial notes](https://gaudi.museum/en/discover-gaudi/antoni-gaudi/centenary-2026/)).

The height record versus Ulm Minster had already flipped when earlier cross elements pushed the basilica past Ulm’s **161.53 m** in late 2025; February 2026 locked the **final** designed height rather than inventing a new programme from scratch ([Gaudí Museum](https://gaudi.museum/en/discover-gaudi/antoni-gaudi/centenary-2026/)).

Blessings and papal liturgy in June 2026 were real cultural events. They were not a certificate of practical completion for the whole temple.

## What is still missing

Design and architecture press keep a blunt checklist. Of the **18 towers** in Gaudí’s scheme, reporting in 2026 still counted **four** yet to be built, alongside unfinished pieces of the **Glory façade**, sacristy / cloister elements, baptistery and related portals — some of which collide with unresolved urban planning around the proposed Glory stair and neighbouring housing ([Domus](https://www.domusweb.it/en/news/2026/07/15/sagrada-familia-completion-demolitions-barcelona.html); [Gaudí Museum](https://gaudi.museum/en/news/is-the-sagrada-familia-finished/)).

Chief-architect commentary quoted in centennial coverage spoke of on the order of **a decade** more for major remaining pieces, not a 2026 ribbon-cutting for the entire complex ([Gaudí Museum](https://gaudi.museum/en/discover-gaudi/antoni-gaudi/centenary-2026/)). Ticket-funded, donation-driven construction has always moved with income; that funding model is why dates slip without the project being “cancelled”.

## Why stone and geometry still dominate the conversation

Gaudí’s language of ruled surfaces, hyperboloids and column trees is not ornamental trivia — it is a fabrication problem. Twisted columns, double-curved panels and ceramic terminals demand:

- accurate **3D coordination** before moulds are cut
- piece drawings that fabricators can nest
- tolerance strategies when historic fabric meets new structure
- clear separation between **design models** and **shop sets**

That is familiar territory for anyone producing [marble shop drawings](/marble.html) or complex cladding packages. The Sagrada Família simply runs the problem for 140+ years in public.

Modern phases have leaned on digital modelling and prefabrication far more than Gaudí’s first decades did — which is why a German-fabricated cross could be stacked in pieces on a high platform. Digital tools do not remove the need for human checking; they raise the cost of a wrong file version.

## Communication lessons for clients and contractors

The 2026 confusion — *height finished* vs *building finished* — maps cleanly onto everyday project risks:

1. **Milestone language must be scoped.** “Structure topped out” is not “handover.” Put the distinction in the drawing issue stamp and in the client email.
2. **Marketing timelines are not fabrication calendars.** A centennial date can drive ceremony; it cannot invent missing urban permits.
3. **Percent-complete figures need a basis.** “70% complete” only helps if everyone knows whether that means cost, volume, or critical-path packages ([Gaudí Museum](https://gaudi.museum/en/news/is-the-sagrada-familia-finished/)).
4. **Long programmes need archival discipline.** Generations of architects have touched this site. Your villa renovation will not last 144 years, but staff will still turn over mid-project — keep DWG masters and revision tables boringly consistent via solid [AutoCAD drafting](/services.html#drafting) practice.
5. **Visuals should match the current issue.** [3D renders](/services.html#3d) that show a completed Glory façade while the permit is unresolved create the same social-media mismatch Barcelona saw in June.

## What drafters outside Barcelona should copy

You may never detail a basilica tower. You will absolutely face clients who think a hero image equals a buildable package. Borrow these habits from the Sagrada story:

- Issue sheets labelled **for information**, **for approval**, and **for fabrication** — never mix them silently.
- When a symbolic milestone lands (topping out, façade dry-in, first stone delivery), publish a one-page **remaining scope** list the same day.
- For stone and ceramic, require **sample approval** against the same piece IDs used on elevations.
- Budget time for **urban and neighbour constraints** the way Barcelona must still confront the Glory stair debate ([Domus](https://www.domusweb.it/en/news/2026/07/15/sagrada-familia-completion-demolitions-barcelona.html)).
- Keep public copy aligned with the drawing set your fabricator holds — D3DDD’s [website design](/website-design.html) work for contractors exists partly so marketing pages stop promising packages the shop drawings have not frozen.

## Height is a number; completion is a checklist

172.5 m is a clean number for headlines. Completion is a checklist of towers, façades, chapels, accessibility, and city permissions. Gaudí’s project remains the world’s most famous argument for patience in architecture — and for honesty in how we describe progress.

If your team is coordinating complex stone, cladding or interior geometry on a long programme, treat every viral milestone as a prompt to re-issue the remaining-works list. That habit is more valuable than another congratulatory post.


## How a century-long site still teaches modern CAD practice

Sagrada Família’s archive problem is extreme, but smaller projects share the pattern: consultants change, software versions change, and someone on site opens an obsolete plot. Practical counters:

- Store **PDF + DWG** of every issued revision in one dated folder.
- Never overwrite; always increment.
- Put the **revision letter on every sheet**, not only the cover.
- When a public milestone lands, sync the marketing site and the [contact](/contact.html) FAQ so clients hear one story.
- For ceramic and stone prototypes, photograph approved samples against the piece ID used on the elevation — the same discipline D3DDD uses on [marble shop drawings](/marble.html).

Gaudí’s geometry rewards teams who treat the model as a fabrication contract, not a sculpture gallery. That is as true for a Barcelona tower cross as for a Dubai reception desk with a waterfall edge.

## Reading the next decade of headlines

Expect more ceremonies as remaining towers and façades complete in stages. Each celebration will invite the same mistake: equating a photogenic moment with practical completion. Keep a printed remaining-works checklist next to the plotter. When the next viral post says “finished,” check the list before you congratulate the client — or before you order the next crate of stone.


The basilica remains an expiatory temple funded largely by visitors and donors, which is why its calendar has always been elastic. That funding model is not a failure of project management so much as a different contract with time — one that modern fixed-price fit-outs cannot copy blindly, but can still admire for archival patience.
